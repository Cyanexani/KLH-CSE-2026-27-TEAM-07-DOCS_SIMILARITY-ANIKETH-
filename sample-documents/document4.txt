The Printing Press

Before the middle of the fifteenth century, almost every book in Europe was copied by hand. Scribes, many of them monks, spent months or even years producing a single volume, and books were so expensive that only churches, universities and wealthy families could own them.

Around 1440, Johannes Gutenberg, a goldsmith from the German city of Mainz, began to develop a new way of printing. His key idea was movable metal type: individual letters cast in a hard alloy of lead, tin and antimony that could be arranged into lines, locked into a frame, inked and pressed onto paper. When a page was finished, the letters could be taken apart and reused for the next one. Gutenberg also developed an oil based ink that stuck well to metal, and he adapted the screw press that was already used for making wine and olive oil.

The most famous product of his workshop is the Gutenberg Bible, completed around 1455. Roughly one hundred and eighty copies were printed, and about fifty of them still survive today in libraries and museums.

The new technology spread with remarkable speed. By the year 1500, printing shops were operating in more than two hundred cities, and millions of books had been produced. Prices fell sharply, literacy rose, and ideas could now travel across a whole continent in a matter of weeks rather than decades. Scientists could share their observations accurately, scholars could compare identical copies of the same text, and religious and political pamphlets reached ordinary readers for the first time.

Many historians consider the printing press one of the most important inventions of the second millennium, because it made knowledge cheaper, more reliable and far easier to share.
