The Water Cycle

The water cycle describes how water moves continuously between the oceans, the atmosphere and the land. It has no real starting point, but it is easiest to follow from the sea. Energy from the sun warms the surface of the ocean, and some of the water turns into vapour and rises into the air. This process is called evaporation, and it lifts an enormous amount of water every single day.

Plants add even more moisture to the air through a process known as transpiration. Water drawn up from the soil by the roots escapes as vapour through tiny pores in the leaves. Together, evaporation and transpiration are often grouped under the single name evapotranspiration.

As warm, moist air rises, it expands and cools. Cooler air cannot hold as much water vapour, so the vapour condenses around small particles of dust, smoke or salt to form tiny droplets. Billions of these droplets gathered together become the clouds we see in the sky. When the droplets collide and grow heavy enough, they fall back to the ground as precipitation, which may be rain, snow, sleet or hail depending on the temperature of the air.

Once the water reaches the ground, it can follow several different paths. Some of it flows over the surface as runoff and collects in streams, rivers and lakes that eventually carry it back to the sea. Some of it soaks into the soil through infiltration and slowly moves down to become groundwater, which can remain underground for thousands of years. In cold regions, water may be stored for a very long time as ice in glaciers and polar ice sheets.

The water cycle is essential for life on Earth. It delivers fresh water to plants, animals and people, it shapes the landscape through erosion, and it moves heat around the planet, which helps to control the weather and the climate. Human activities such as cutting down forests, building cities and burning fossil fuels are changing how quickly water moves through each stage of the cycle, which makes understanding it more important than ever.
