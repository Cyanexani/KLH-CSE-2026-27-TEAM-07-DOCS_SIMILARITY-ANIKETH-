Rainfall and Farming

Farmers have always watched the sky. The amount of rain that falls during a season decides whether seeds sprout, whether crops grow strong and whether there will be enough grain to store for the winter. To understand why rainfall changes from year to year, it helps to look at where rain actually comes from.

As warm, moist air rises, it expands and cools. Cooler air cannot hold as much water vapour, so the vapour condenses around small particles of dust, smoke or salt to form tiny droplets. Billions of these droplets gathered together become the clouds we see in the sky. When the droplets collide and grow heavy enough, they fall back to the ground as precipitation, which may be rain, snow, sleet or hail depending on the temperature of the air.

For a farmer, what happens after the rain lands matters just as much. Some of it flows over the surface as runoff and collects in streams, rivers and lakes that eventually carry it back to the sea. Some of it soaks into the soil through infiltration and slowly moves down to become groundwater, which can remain underground for thousands of years. Runoff that moves too fast can wash away the fertile top layer of a field, while water that soaks in slowly keeps the roots of crops supplied between storms.

Good farming practice tries to slow water down. Contour ploughing, terraces on steep hillsides and strips of grass along the edges of fields all reduce runoff and give the soil more time to absorb each shower. Mulch and cover crops protect the surface from the impact of heavy drops, and ponds or small reservoirs store surplus water for dry months.

Irrigation has allowed farming to spread into regions where rainfall alone is not enough, but it comes at a cost. Pumping groundwater faster than infiltration can refill it lowers the water table, and poorly drained irrigated land can slowly become salty. Careful planning of when and how much to water is therefore one of the most valuable skills in modern agriculture.
